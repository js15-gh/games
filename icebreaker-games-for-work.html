For a team, in ten minutes

Icebreaker Games for Work

Most workplace icebreakers fail for the same reason: they ask people to perform. Stand up, share something personal, be funny on demand — in front of colleagues, with a manager watching. The people who enjoy that were never the problem.

What works instead is a game with a real decision in it. Something you can be right or wrong about, where the interesting thing about you comes out sideways rather than because you were asked to volunteer it.

What to look for

  • Everyone acts at once. No going round the circle. Nobody rehearsing their turn instead of listening.
  • Nothing to install. Asking a team to download an app for a ten-minute icebreaker is how you lose half of them at the door.
  • A clean ending. It should finish in ten to twenty minutes without anybody having to call time on it.
  • Nothing personal unless volunteered. Good icebreakers do not ask about money, families or beliefs.

Four that hold up

Two Truths and a Lie — 3 to 12 The classic, with the tedious parts removed. Everyone types their three at the same time instead of the room waiting; the phone shuffles them so the lie is not sitting where its author put it; and there is a score, which is what turns it from a round of introductions into a game. Best on a first day or a new team. This or That — 3 to 16 One person answers a question where nobody can see, and everyone else guesses what they said. Perfect for a team that has been remote for a year: it measures how well you actually know each other, and the gaps are funny rather than awkward. One Word — 3 to 8 Everybody writes one clue for the same word, and any two clues that match are thrown away before the guesser sees them. Co-operative, so there is no winner to resent, and it is a genuinely good test of whether a group thinks alike. Ten minutes for a full round. The Dial — 4 to 12 in teams One person sees a hidden spot between Underrated and Overrated and names something that sits exactly there. Their team argues about where that is. It gets a room talking to each other rather than to the facilitator, which is usually the actual goal.

Over a video call

All of these work on a call, because the game itself never depends on hearing anybody — the phone or the second browser tab carries the private half, and the call carries the arguing. Share the link in the chat, everyone opens it, and the screen share is only needed if you want one.

This or That and Most Likely To are the two that survive a bad connection best: a round is one tap and one reveal.

A practical note on running one

Set it up before people arrive, not while they watch. Start a lobby, add the team once, and every game you open puts everyone straight into their seats — so the icebreaker starts in ten seconds rather than after four minutes of typing names and reading a code out twice.

Keep family mode on. It is the default, and on a work team it is simply the right setting.

No accounts, nothing collected. These run in a browser with no sign-up and no email. Nothing anybody types goes anywhere except the game they are playing, and it is deleted within a day. See the privacy page.
