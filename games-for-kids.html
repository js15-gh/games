For younger players

Games for Kids

The problem with an hour of videos isn't the screen. It's that nothing is being asked of the child. These are all group games — everyone in the room, each on a phone, talking to each other rather than at a feed.

No accounts, no sign-ups, nothing collected. Children don't type an email anywhere on this site, and there's nothing to log into. A parent can hand over a phone without thinking about it.

Ages 6 to 8 — with a grown-up playing too

At this age the game needs to be forgiving, turn-based, and impossible to lose badly.

Antakshari

Perfect for the youngest players because knowing one song is enough to contribute, and the phone handles the letter, the score and remembering what's been sung. Children who can't yet read a scoreboard can still take a full turn.

What it asks of them: listening for the last sound in a word, recalling songs on demand, and waiting for a turn.

Play AntakshariTeams · Hindi or English letters

Tambola

Number recognition dressed as a party game. A six-year-old scanning a ticket for 47 is doing genuine work, and they win exactly as often as the adults, which is the whole appeal.

What it asks of them: reading two-digit numbers quickly and tracking a grid.

Play Tambola2–30 players · a ticket on every phone

Ages 9 to 12 — the sweet spot

Ito

The best game on this site for this age, and it isn't close. Each player holds a secret number from 1 to 100 and has to describe it through a theme — "how scary is the animal" — without saying it. Then everyone plays in order, lowest first.

It is, quietly, an exercise in estimation and relative scale: is a crocodile a 90 or a 70, and how do you say that so someone else can place it? Children who freeze at "estimate the answer" in a maths lesson will do it happily here for an hour.

What it asks of them: ranking things on a scale, describing a quantity without naming it, and listening carefully enough to place their own card.

Play Ito2–10 players · 100 themes across 10 categories

Dumb Charades

Non-verbal communication under time pressure, which is a real skill and one children are often better at than adults. The film shows only on the acting player's phone, so nobody has to whisper it or leave the room.

Play Dumb CharadesTwo teams · the other team picks the film

Ages 13 and up

Judgement (Kachuful)

Worth singling out, because a card game teaches things a quiz can't. To bid well you have to estimate how many tricks a hand is worth, watch which cards have gone, and revise as the round unfolds — probability and inference, without anyone calling it that.

The reason it's usually out of reach for a beginner is that a table of experienced players won't wait while someone learns. Here the phone greys out the cards they can't legally play and says why, so a thirteen-year-old can join a real game on the first hand.

Play Judgement3–7 players · learn as you play

Bollywood Jeopardy

Splits by decade, which is the fun of it: pick the 1970s and the grandparents suddenly know everything. Cross-generational games are rarer than they should be.

Play Bollywood JeopardyTeams · pick a decade

A note for parents

None of these work alone — they all need two or more people in a room, which is the point. If you're looking for something a child can do by themselves for forty minutes, this isn't it, and honestly there are better things for that than a games site.

Nothing here has advertising aimed at children, in-app purchases, or a login. If we ever add something aimed specifically at younger players, it'll be built the same way.

Every game here is free, runs in a browser, and needs no app or sign-up. One person creates a game, shares the link, everyone plays on their own phone.
