Ten, fifteen, twenty people

Party Games for Large Groups

Most party games quietly assume six people. Past about ten they stop working — not because the rules break, but because everyone spends most of the evening waiting for their turn, and a game where you act once every twelve minutes is a game people drift away from.

What actually goes wrong at fifteen

Three things, and they are worth naming because they tell you what to look for:

  • Turn queues. If the game is one-person-at-a-time, fifteen players means fourteen people watching. Anything built around a single active player needs to be short.
  • Shouting. Big rooms are loud. Games that depend on hearing what someone said fall apart at exactly the point the party gets good.
  • Latecomers. Somebody always arrives an hour in. A game that cannot absorb them leaves you either restarting or leaving them out.

The way round all three is simultaneous play: everyone acting at once, on their own phone, with the result revealed together. Nobody queues, nothing depends on hearing, and the next round is always a clean place to add a person.

The ones that scale

Tambola (Housie) — up to 30 The classic large-group game for a reason: everyone plays every single call. A real three-by-nine ticket on every phone and claims checked against the numbers actually called, so nobody has to adjudicate a wrong claim in front of thirty people. Most Likely To — 3 to 16 Everyone votes at once and the votes stay hidden until the last one is in, so nobody follows the room. Rounds are about ninety seconds, which means a latecomer waits ninety seconds. This or That — 3 to 16 One person answers, everyone else guesses what they said. It looks like a poll and becomes an argument about who really knows whom. The Round Table — 3 to 20 A live secret ballot for Traitors-style games. Everyone votes from their own phone and the table reveals together — which is the whole reason it works at twenty, where a show of hands is just the first three people deciding for everybody. Dumb Charades — 4 to 20 Two teams, so half the room is always doing something. The opposing team types the film and it shows only on the actor's phone: no whispering, nobody sent out of the room. Bollywood Jeopardy — 2 to 20 in teams Teams keep the numbers manageable, and it is turn-based rather than a buzzer race, so the loudest person does not win by default.

The ones to avoid at this size

Worth saying plainly. Judgement and Kaali Tiri are card games with a hard ceiling — seven and ten players — and squeezing more in ruins them. Cipher and The Dial are team games that get slower as the teams grow; twelve is the sensible top. And anything co-operative, like In Sync or One Word, gets harder in a way that stops being fun past eight or so.

None of that is a flaw. Split a big room into two games rather than forcing twenty people into one that wants six.

Do the names once

The genuinely tedious part of a big evening is not the games, it is typing fifteen names into each one. Start a lobby instead: add everybody once, and every game you open puts the same people straight into their seats. One link, shared once, works all night.

A note on phones. Everything here runs in a browser — no app, no sign-up, nothing to install on fifteen phones at the door. One person opens a game, shares the link, and everyone else is in.
